\item \textbf{1st Scenario: User Registration and Profile Management}\\
Elena Rossi, an avid cyclist and member of a local biking association, decides to join the BBP platform to keep track of her cycling activity and share information with other bikers. 
Upon opening the application, she selects the \textit{Sign Up} option. BBP prompts her to enter her personal details such as name, email, and password. Once the verification process is complete, Elena accesses her personal profile page. She updates her information, adds a short description of her cycling interests, and enables location permissions. From this moment, Elena becomes a registered user, able to record her trips and contribute data about bike paths.

\item \textbf{2nd Scenario: Recording and Viewing a Trip with Statistics}\\
Fausto Coppi is training for his next Tour. Fausto decides to record his rides using BBP. Before starting, he opens the app and presses \textit{Start Trip Recording}. 
During the ride, the app collects GPS data to track his route and speed. Once he arrives at his destination, he presses \textit{Stop}. The system automatically saves his trip and calculates key statistics: total distance, average speed, and travel time.  Fausto receives an automatic summary of the detected path and potential obstacles identified by the sensors. He notices that BBP has mistakenly flagged a bump as a pothole, so he corrects the information before confirming the path. Once validated, the path is saved in his account.
Fausto later accesses his trip history, where he can review all his recorded rides, compare performances, and track his progress over time.

\item \textbf{3rd Scenario: Manual Insertion of Path Information}\\
During one of her daily rides, Federica Conti—an environmental engineering student and urban cyclist—remembers that a particular area of the city has no documented cycling paths on BBP’s map. Curious and motivated to improve the platform’s coverage, she decides to explore the zone herself.
While riding through the neighborhood, Federica discovers a newly built cycling lane that is not yet available on BBP. Wanting to record it before she forgets, she opens the \textit{Manual insertion section} and manually inserts all the relevant details: the street names, total distance, surface quality, and current condition (e.g., Optimal). She also reports a minor obstacle: a small pothole near a crossroad.
However, due to the lack of internet connection in that area, she is unable to upload the contribution immediately. Instead, BBP’s local storage feature allows her to save the path as a local draft associated with her account.
Once she returns home and reconnects to the internet, the application automatically synchronizes the previously saved trip with her BBP account. After reviewing the details one more time, Federica decides to make the path public. The system then updates the platform, publishing the new cycling lane for all users to see.


\item \textbf{4th Scenario: Publishing a Recently Completed Path}\\
After reviewing and confirming the data from her latest ride, Sofia Moretti, an experienced city biker, decides to share it immediately with other cyclists. 
As soon as she finishes the trip and validates the automatically collected information, BBP prompts her with an option to \textit{Publish Path Information}. 
Sofia confirms the publication request, and the system instantly adds the genereted contributions to the public database, updating the collaborative map of available routes. 
Within moments, the new path becomes visible to all users of the platform.

\item \textbf{5th Scenario: Publishing a Previously Stored Path}\\
Two weeks later, Andrea Ricci, a cycling enthusiast who often explores rural routes, revisits one of his older recorded trips, which he never published.
He clicks \textit{Publish}, and BBP processes the data, making it available in the shared inventory. The system automatically merges his contribution with existing information about the same route, if available, ensuring that the most recent and confirmed data are displayed.

\item \textbf{6th Scenario: Consulting and Choosing a Path (Registered User)}\\
Francesco Leone, a registered cyclist who commutes daily by bike, plans a weekend trip across the city. He logs into BBP and specifies his origin and destination. 
The system searches the database for suitable routes and displays multiple options on the map sorted by their score, considering factors such as safety, condition, and efficiency. 
Since Francesco’s profile indicates a preference for smoother surfaces and minimal traffic, BBP’s recommendation system automatically prioritizes such routes in the results. 
After reviewing the suggested paths, Francesco selects one and saves it as his chosen itinerary for the day.

\item \textbf{7th Scenario: Consulting and Choosing a Path (Unregistered User)}\\
Marta Villa, a casual cyclist who enjoys leisure rides on weekends, wants to discover new paths without creating an account. She opens the BBP app without logging in and accesses the public map. 
BBP displays all available paths but without personalized recommendations. Marta browses the map, filters by \textit{Optimal} status, and selects a route along the lakeside. Although she cannot record trips or add data, she can still view general information, such as total length, average condition, and user comments.

\item \textbf{8th Scenario: Updating Information on an Existing Path}\\
During a weekly commute, Tommaso Greco, a frequent user of BBP, rides along a route previously marked as \textit{Optimal}. He notices that a section of the track has recently deteriorated and requires maintenance. 
Once he finishes the ride, he opens BBP and selects \textit{Update Path Information}. He searches for the existing path and updates its condition from \textit{Optimal} to \textit{Requires Maintenance}. 
The system records his update and automatically aggregates it with reports from other users who have recently traveled the same route. 
By considering both the number of confirmations and the recency of the available data, BBP determines the most reliable current status of the path and updates its classification accordingly.

\end{itemize}

The entities interacting with the system are organized around a hierarchical user structure that reflects the different capabilities exposed by the application. At the top, the abstract \textit{User} class generalizes every individual accessing the platform, while \textit{RegisteredUser} extends it with attributes such as \texttt{userID}, \texttt{email}, \texttt{password}, \texttt{name}, and \texttt{surname}, enabling trip recording, contribution management, and profile customization. \textit{UnregisteredUser}, still a specialization of \textit{User}, represents visitors who can browse the map and search paths without owning trips or publishing contributions; both types of users interact with the map-based exploration of the cycling network, but only registered users are associated with a history of trips through the \textit{Trip} entity.

​

The core dynamic element of the model is the \textit{Trip} class, which represents a cycling session performed by a registered user and identified by \texttt{tripId}. Trips are specialized into \textit{ManualTrip} and \textit{AutomaticTrip}, depending on whether they are manually specified or recorded through sensors, with \textit{AutomaticTrip} storing temporal and quantitative data such as \texttt{startTime}, \texttt{endTime}, \texttt{distance}, \texttt{avgTripSpeed}, \texttt{maxSpeed}, \texttt{elevation}, and an optional \texttt{weatherSummary}, and being associated with \textit{Sensors} that provide GPS and motion readings. Each trip is mapped onto the infrastructure via one or more \textit{StreetSegment} instances, modeling individual pieces of road between intersections, and for each traversed street segment the trip can generate at most one associated \textit{Contribution}, resulting in a trip-level collection of contributions, each focusing on a single street segment crossed during the ride.

​

The \textit{Path} class models higher-level routes composed of multiple \textit{StreetSegment} entities, and is characterized by attributes such as \texttt{pathId}, \texttt{length}, and \texttt{avgScore}. A street segment is defined through \texttt{name}, its endpoints \texttt{startLocation} and \texttt{endLocation} of type \textit{Coordinate}, a geometric polyline \texttt{streetPath}, a local \texttt{score}, and a set of \texttt{mergedInfo} items that summarize aggregated information, including whether the segment is currently considered part of the cycling network via attributes such as \texttt{bikeLane}, \texttt{speedLimit}, and \texttt{bikePath}. In this model, a street segment is the atomic infrastructure unit: each \textit{Contribution} always refers to exactly one \textit{StreetSegment}, and the system periodically merges all contributions linked to that segment in order to update its score, its merged information, and ultimately its visibility in paths and map-based queries. When a segment becomes a bike path according to the merged data, it is highlighted on the map and becomes eligible to be part of the paths suggested to users when searching for routes between two locations.

​

A \textit{Contribution} encapsulates any piece of information about the cycling network generated during trips or via manual reports. It includes metadata such as \texttt{id}, \texttt{creationTime}, \texttt{title}, \texttt{description}, and flags \texttt{isPublished} and \texttt{isAutomatic}, as well as an optional qualitative \texttt{status} of type \textit{Status}, ranging from \texttt{NONE} to \texttt{OPTIMAL}, \texttt{MEDIUM}, \texttt{SUFFICIENT}, and \\\texttt{REQUIRES\_MAINTENANCE}. Each contribution also maintains a list of \textit{Information} elements (\texttt{info: List<Information>}) describing the actual content of the report; among these, a distinguished group is represented by the abstract \textit{BikePathSuitability} subclass, which includes attributes \texttt{isSuitable} and \texttt{reasonDetails} and is further specialized into concrete classes such as \textit{SpeedLimitUpdate}, \textit{BikeLaneUpdate}, and \textit{HeavyTraffic}. When a contribution concerns a street segment that is not currently marked as bike path, ordinary information items (for example obstacles or roadworks) are immediately discarded during merging, whereas \textit{BikePathSuitability} information is temporarily retained: the system periodically retrieves all contributions from different users for that segment and only after a given time window, if multiple coherent suitability items support the same conclusion (for instance, consistent speed-limit or bike-lane updates), it may promote the segment to bike path instead of discarding them. In this way, bike-path suitability information acts as a special trigger influencing both the boolean \texttt{bikePath} attribute of the street segment and the computation of path suggestions, while generic information only refines the segment’s score and maintenance status.

​

Infrastructure-related facts are modeled by the abstract \textit{Information} class, which records \\ \texttt{creationTime} and \texttt{position} (a \textit{Coordinate} described by \texttt{latitude} and \texttt{longitude}), and is specialized not only by \textit{BikePathSuitability} but also by other concrete subclasses. These include \textit{Warning}, \textit{Event}, \textit{Obstacle}, \textit{Animal}, and \textit{Roadwork}, which respectively store a textual \texttt{message}, an event \texttt{name} and validity interval, an obstacle \texttt{type}, an \texttt{animal} \texttt{species} with a flag indicating whether it is wild, and the \texttt{responsibleAuthority} plus temporal bounds for roadworks. Such information items are attached to contributions and, after merging, to street segments as part of \texttt{mergedInfo}, providing a structured and typed view of the network’s conditions that supports both the computation of scores and the decision about whether a non-bike street segment can be upgraded to bike path.

​

Finally, the sensing and external-service layer is abstracted by the \textit{Sensors} and \textit{WeatherService} entities. \textit{Sensors}, characterized by \texttt{sensorId}, \texttt{type}, and \texttt{samplingFrequency}, are used by automatic trips to acquire motion and positioning data, which underpin automatic contribution creation, obstacle inference, and interruption detection. The \textit{WeatherService} represents an external provider contacted by the system to enrich automatic trips with contextual weather information, explaining the presence of the \texttt{weatherSummary} attribute in \textit{AutomaticTrip}; together with user-generated contributions and special \textit{BikePathSuitability} items, these elements allow the system to maintain an up-to-date, bike-centric representation of the road network that dynamically highlights and exploits suitable segments when answering path search requests.

This state diagram models the lifecycle of a user account within the application, from the initial unregistered state to registration, authentication, and eventual termination. This lifecycle ensures secure onboarding and persistent access control, while supporting error handling and explicit account dismissal.

The automatic trip workflow begins when the user simply presses a button to start recording. While the user is moving, the application continuously collects sensor and GPS data; if the user stops for a significant amount of time, the system automatically detects the lack of movement and pauses the recording, resuming it as soon as the ride restarts. At any moment, the user can explicitly terminate the trip by pressing the stop button, or decide to discard the recording, in which case no trip or contribution is stored. When a trip is successfully concluded, the system automatically generates a draft contribution populated with the information inferred from the recorded data. The user can review and edit this contribution, then either save it only in the personal trip history or publish it so that it becomes visible to other users; publication can also be postponed, allowing the user to keep the contribution private and decide later whether to make it public.

The manual trip workflow enables the user to specify not only the start and end points of their journey, but also the precise roads and paths actually taken during the ride. Throughout this process, the user is able to document issues encountered—such as potholes or other obstacles—along the exact path. \subsubsection{StreetSegment}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{RASD/Version1.0/images/street_segment_state_diagram.png}
        \caption{StreetSegment.}
        \label{fig:street_segment_state_diagram}%
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

The street segment state diagram models how a road segment is progressively considered, monitored, and possibly dismissed as part of the cycling network. Each segment starts in the ‘NotBikePath’ state and moves to ‘CandidateBikePath’ when it receives the first BikePathSuitability information derived from user contributions. During an observation period, the system keeps collecting suitability information: if, at the end, the accumulated BikePathSuitability is sufficiently positive and consistent, the segment is promoted to the ‘BikePath’ state; otherwise, if suitability is not confirmed, the segment transitions directly to ‘Inactive’, meaning it is no longer considered for cycling purposes.

Once in the ‘BikePath’ state, the segment continues to receive contributions, including BikePathSuitability items and other Information such as obstacles or roadworks, and these trigger a transition to ‘BikePathUnderReview’ whenever an additional evaluation is required. In this review state, the system aggregates incoming suitability reports: if negative reports are not confirmed or are compensated by positive ones, the segment returns to ‘BikePath’, updating its merged information with any new obstacles or events while still being considered suitable; if, instead, consistent negative reports indicate that the segment is no longer suitable as a bike path, it moves to the ‘Inactive’ state. A direct transition from ‘BikePath’ to ‘Inactive’ also exists to model administrative decisions or permanent closures, and even after a segment has become ‘Inactive’, the arrival of new BikePathSuitability information can restart the cycle by allowing it to be reconsidered as a ‘CandidateBikePath’.

\section{Product Functions}
\label{sec:product_functions}
The BBP platform is designed to support cyclists in the collection, exploration, and sharing of information about bike paths. It provides functionalities that cover every stage of the cycling experience, from trip recording and path contribution to map consultation and route recommendation. By integrating automated sensor detection, user-generated data, and aggregation mechanisms, the platform ensures reliable, up-to-date information for all users, offering a seamless and collaborative riding experience.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sign Up and Profile Management}\\
    Users create an account on the platform to access advanced functionalities, such as trip recording and contribution publishing. During registration, they provide personal information and configure basic preferences (e.g., cycling style or comfort priorities). Registered users can later update their profile, manage privacy settings, and modify their preferences, which are used by the recommendation system to tailor route suggestions.

   \item \textbf{Trip Recording and Storage}\\
Registered users can record their bike trips through the automatic tracking option. 
During a ride, the system collects essential trip data such as GPS position, speed, and timestamps. 
Once the trip ends, the user can save it to their personal history, where BBP generates statistical summaries including total distance, average speed, travel time, and performance indicators. 
These statistics allow cyclists to review past activity and monitor their progress over time.

    \item \textbf{Manual Insertion of Path Information}\\
    Users can manually report details about bike paths that are missing or incomplete in the shared database. This includes route description, path condition (e.g., Optimal, Medium, Requires Maintenance), and obstacles encountered. The system stores submitted information as a contribution that can later be published to the public map.

    \item \textbf{Automatic Path Detection and Confirmation}\\
    BBP supports automatic acquisition of path information through mobile sensors.
    When activated, the system uses GPS, accelerometer, and gyroscope data to reconstruct the path followed and detect potential obstacles such as potholes or irregularities. Detected information is presented to the user for review: registered users must confirm or correct each detection before it becomes a valid contribution.

    \item \textbf{Publication of Path Information}\\
    Registered users can publish both manually inserted data and confirmed automatic contributions.
    Publishing makes the information visible to all platform users and allows it to affect the global classification, condition score, and visualization of the corresponding bike path. Users may publish contributions immediately after a trip or at a later moment by reviewing stored entries.

    \item \textbf{Bike Path Consultation and Map Navigation}\\
    BBP enables both registered and unregistered users to browse a map of available cycling paths.
    Users can search for routes between a chosen origin and destination. The system displays viable paths, enriched with condition evaluations, obstacles, and contributions from the community. Maps remain accessible even without an account, though personalized features are limited to registered users.

    \item \textbf{Personalized Route Recommendation}\\
    The platform automatically generates a list of suitable paths between two points by analyzing path conditions, traffic comfort, safety indicators, and user preferences (for registered users only).
    The recommendation engine ranks each path by score, which considers contributions, surface smoothness, obstacle frequency, and how effectively the path leads to the destination. Registered users receive more personalized suggestions based on their saved profile settings.

    \item \textbf{Data Aggregation and Reconciliation}\\
    BBP aggregates contributions from multiple users who traveled the same path.
    The system applies a prevalence strategy, combining the number of confirmations, recency of reports, and consistency of information to derive the most reliable status for each path segment. This aggregated evaluation determines the official condition displayed on the map.

    \item \textbf{Weather Data Enrichment}\\
    For each recorded trip, BBP can request meteorological data from external services, such as weather conditions, temperature, and wind intensity. This information is integrated into the trip summary, enabling cyclists to better understand the context of their performance or encountered difficulties.

\section{User Characteristics}
\begin{itemize}
The BBP platform is designed for two main categories of users, each with different goals, levels of interaction, and technical proficiency.
\item \textbf{Registered Users}\\
Registered cyclists use the platform to record their trips, contribute information about bike paths, and access personalized features such as tailored route recommendations. They are typically familiar with mobile applications, GPS-based services, and standard interaction patterns such as registration, profile configuration, and map navigation. Their technical proficiency is generally medium to high, although the system is designed to remain accessible for less experienced users. Their motivation is to track their cycling activity, improve route planning, and contribute to the quality of shared path information. They may interact with the platform on a daily or weekly basis, especially when using BBP as part of their commuting routine or during periods of regular training.

\item \textbf{Unregistered Users}\\
Unregistered users access BBP to explore available bike paths without creating an account. Their interaction is limited to map browsing, viewing path details, and performing basic searches between an origin and destination. These users vary widely in their technical abilities, ranging from casual cyclists with minimal experience using navigation apps to proficient riders looking for quick route information. Their engagement frequency is typically low, consisting of occasional use on weekends or when planning new itineraries. Since they cannot record trips or publish data, their expectations focus primarily on a simple, intuitive map interface and clear visualization of path conditions.

\item \textbf{Cross-User Expectations}\\
All users expect BBP to provide:
a simple and intuitive interface, accessible even to those with limited technical skills; reliable and up-to-date information on path conditions, obstacles, and safety; fast and accurate map navigation and trip visualization; privacy-respecting handling of location and sensor data. The system accommodates diverse levels of cycling expertise and device familiarity, ensuring accessibility and usability across the entire user base.

\end{itemize}

\newcommand{\cda}{\theda\stepcounter{da}}
    \textbf{[DA\cda]} The urban cycling infrastructure can be accurately represented by a finite set of interconnected paths.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Each path corresponds to a real, navigable route that cyclists can take within the target city or area.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Users always interact with the system through a device with a reliable Internet connection.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Users who sign up to the platform possess a valid and active email address.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]}  Users can access the mailbox of the email address provided during registration.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Registered Users own a mobile device equipped with GPS, accelerometer, and gyroscope sensors.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} When automatic tracking is enabled, the mobile device’s sensors operate correctly and provide accurate data samples.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Users do not intentionally provide false information about bike paths, obstacles, or conditions.
    %chiedere al prof, dato che potenzialmente facciamo i controlli con varie soglie e cheidere anche dei controlli pdfpgp


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Obstacles detected by the sensors during a trip correspond to actual modifications or irregularities of the physical path. %chiedere se ridondante


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} The external meteorological services queried by BBP always return correct and timely information based on geographical coordinates and timestamps.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]}  Map data and geolocation services used by BBP are available and functioning when the system computes paths between origin and destination.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Users who manually insert or edit path information correctly identify the streets and segments they intend to describe.
    % da chiedere sempre stessa questione


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} Users who confirm or correct automatically detected obstacles provide honest and meaningful feedback, improving the reliability of aggregated contributions.
    %chiedere


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} The timestamps associated with trips and contributions reflect the actual moment in which the data was collected.


    \textbf{[DA\cda]} When the system aggregates contributions, the recency and consistency rules are meaningful because users submit updates promptly after observing path changes.
